Monster High

Below are the characters and their mythological, folkloric, and literary influences:

Draculaura

- Dracula (Bram Stoker - 1897): Draculaura is the Count’s adopted daughter.
- Carmilla (Sheridan Le Fanu - 1872): In the original Generation 1 (G1) timeline, Draculaura’s biological mother is named Camille (or Carmilla).
- Béla Lugosi (Dracula - 1931): The character’s vocal portrayal in the original animated series features a Hungarian accent directly inspired by Béla Lugosi’s performance.
- Nosferatu (1922): In more recent generations (G3), her teddy bear is named Noss Berato.
- The Roman Empire and Dacia: According to official Generation 1 diaries, Draculaura was born human around 412 AD in the former Roman province of Dacia (present-day Romania) to a Roman soldier named Gaius.
- Eastern European Vampire Folklore: Draculaura possesses several traditional weaknesses and powers rooted in Slavic and Romanian myths, such as sleeping in a coffin, having no reflection in mirrors, and the ability to transform into a bat.
- Witchcraft and Occultism (G3): In the franchise’s most recent reboot, her mythology has been expanded to include the practice of secret witchcraft and temporal magic, aligning her more closely with modern folklore surrounding witches, in addition to classic vampirism.

Clawdeen Wolf

- The Wolf Man (1941): Clawdeen is the daughter of a werewolf, a concept that recalls Lawrence Talbot, the character portrayed by Lon Chaney Jr. in the classic horror film The Wolf Man.
- European Werewolf Legends: Medieval European folklore, including the French Loup-garou and the Germanic Werwolf, established many of the traditional characteristics associated with werewolves, such as transformations during the full moon and animalistic instincts.
- Wolf Pack Behavior: Wolves depend on their packs for survival, a concept reflected in Clawdeen's large and lively family, including her siblings Clawd, Howleen, and Clawdia. Her heightened senses and instinctive reactions, such as howling at the moon, also reinforce her wolf-like nature.
- Classic Fairy Tales: Although not a direct adaptation, Clawdeen's aesthetic can be compared to the wolf imagery found in stories such as Little Red Riding Hood and The Three Little Pigs.

Cleo de Nile

- The Mummy (1932): Cleo was originally introduced as the "daughter of the Mummy." The idea of an ancient Egyptian mummy awakening thousands of years after being buried recalls the classic horror film starring Boris Karloff.
- Cleopatra VII: Cleo's name is a shortened form of Cleopatra. Her appearance also evokes Elizabeth Taylor's portrayal of the Egyptian queen in the film Cleopatra (1963).
- Nefertiti: Cleo's older sister, Nefera de Nile, takes her name from Nefertiti, one of ancient Egypt's most famous queens.
- Ramses II and the Nile River: Cleo's father, Ramses de Nile, recalls the powerful pharaoh Ramses II. Their family name is also a play on words referring to the Nile, the river that sustained ancient Egyptian civilization.
- The Uraeus: Cleo's cobra pet, Hissette, and her snake-shaped bracelets evoke the Uraeus, the rearing cobra worn on Egyptian royal crowns as a symbol of sovereignty and divine protection, associated with the goddess Wadjet.
- The Scarab: Scarab motifs in Cleo's accessories recall the sacred beetle associated with Khepri, the Egyptian god connected to the rising sun, transformation, and rebirth.
- Mummification Rituals: The golden bandages that form part of Cleo's outfit reference the linen wrappings used in Egyptian mummification. Some of her accessories also evoke canopic jars, which were used to store the organs of the deceased.
