Shrek

Below are the characters and their mythological, folkloric, and literary influences:

Shrek

- Orcus and European Folklore: The word "ogre" is sometimes associated with Orcus, a Roman deity connected to the underworld and punishment. Shrek's appearance also recalls the traditional European ogre: a gigantic, frightening creature often portrayed as a man-eater.
- Shrek! (1990): Shrek originated in the illustrated children's book by American author William Steig. The character's name comes from the Yiddish word "shrek," related to the German "Schreck," both meaning fear or fright.
- The Brothers Grimm: Steig's story subverts traditional fairy-tale conventions, particularly the idea that heroes and princesses must be beautiful and heroic. The film continues this parody through its references to classic fairy-tale characters.
- Tristan and Isolde: Shrek and Fiona's journey recalls the medieval romance of Tristan and Isolde. In both stories, a man is sent to bring a princess to marry another ruler, but the two develop romantic feelings during the journey.

Donkey

- Shrek! (1990): Donkey first appeared in William Steig's illustrated book, although his appearance and mannerisms in the film were modeled after Perry, a real miniature donkey from California.
- Aesop's Fables and Classical Literature: In ancient literature, donkeys often represent humble and underestimated individuals. This symbolism appears in works such as Aesop's fables and The Golden Ass by Apuleius.
- European Fairy Tales: Donkey's ability to speak recalls traditional stories featuring extraordinary animals, such as The Bremen Town Musicians by the Brothers Grimm.
- Don Quixote: The relationship between Shrek and Donkey resembles that of Don Quixote and Sancho Panza. While Shrek is cynical and practical, Donkey is talkative, optimistic, and constantly pushes his companion into adventures.

Fiona

- The Brothers Grimm: Fiona's story recalls Rapunzel, through her imprisonment in a tower, and Sleeping Beauty, through the curse that can only be broken by true love's kiss.
- The Taming of the Shrew: Fiona and Shrek's relationship, marked by arguments, stubbornness, and growing affection, evokes the structure of Shakespearean romantic comedies.
- Shrek! (1990): In William Steig's original book, Fiona is an extraordinarily ugly princess who falls in love with Shrek because they share the same appearance and unusual tastes. The film transforms this idea into a broader parody of traditional princess stories.
- Beauty and the Beast: Fiona's transformation between human and ogre forms recalls the recurring fairy-tale theme of physical transformation and the contrast between appearance and identity.
- The Matrix (1999) and Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon (2000): Fiona's action scenes incorporate martial arts choreography inspired by these films, contrasting with the traditional image of a princess waiting to be rescued.
